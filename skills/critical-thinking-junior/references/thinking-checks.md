# The Nine Thinking Checks
These are tests for an *answer*, not questions to open with. When the student's answer is thin, work out which check it misses, name it, show the short example, and ask again.

Naming the check teaches the student the tool: *"That's clear, but not precise yet."* Use one check at a time.

Each check has a weak answer and a stronger one. Showing both side by side is the quickest way for a young student to see the difference.

---

## 1. Clarity: "Can I say it more clearly?"
If something isn't clear, nobody can tell whether it's true or useful. This check comes first.

- **Weak:** "My project is about making things better."
- **Stronger:** "My project is about reducing food waste in our school canteen."

**Ask:** Can you say that another way? Can you share an example?

---

## 2. Accuracy: "Is it actually true? How do I know?"
Something can be clear and still be false.

- **Weak:** "Everyone knows we only use 10% of our brains." (This is a well-known myth.)
- **Stronger:** "My Science textbook says ___, and I checked it on a second reliable source."

**Ask:** How do you know that's true? How could you check it? Who said it, and how would they know?

---

## 3. Precision: "Can I be more exact?"
Something can be clear and true but too vague to be useful.

- **Weak:** "I study a lot."
- **Stronger:** "I study about 45 minutes on weekdays and not at all on weekends."

**Ask:** How much? How many? By when? All of them, or just the few you remember?

---

## 4. Relevance: "Does this connect to my question?"
Something can be true and exact but about something else.

- **Weak:** Question: "Should school start later?" Answer: "My cousin's school has a nicer field."
- **Stronger:** "Teenagers' sleep patterns mean many of us aren't fully awake at 7.30am, which affects learning."

**Ask:** How does that help answer your question? If it were different, would your answer change?

---

## 5. Depth: "Am I dealing with the real cause, or just the surface?"
A shallow answer skips the hard parts and doesn't ask *why*.

- **Weak:** "I keep forgetting my homework, so I'll try harder to remember."
- **Stronger:** "I forget because I don't write it down. I'll note it in my planner before leaving each class."

**Ask:** Why does this happen in the first place? What makes it hard? If it were easy, wouldn't it be solved already?

If the problem keeps coming back no matter what, say so: there may be something bigger going on that's worth exploring with a parent or teacher.

---

## 6. Breadth: "Have I looked at it from other angles?"
An answer can go deep into one angle and miss all the others.

- **Weak:** Deciding the class outing by asking only your three closest friends.
- **Stronger:** Also asking classmates who don't usually speak up, someone who can't spend much money, and the form teacher.

**Ask:** What would someone who disagrees say? What would a younger kid ask about this? What would a teacher or parent notice?

---

## 7. Logic: "Do my reasons fit together?"
Each part may be fine on its own but they don't fit together.

- **Weak:** "My grandfather never exercised and lived to 90, so exercise doesn't matter."
- **Stronger:** "One person's story isn't enough to tell what's true for most people. I'd need to look at many people."

Another weak one: "I don't care what my classmates think," followed by spending the whole of recess worrying about what they think.

**Ask:** You said this, and now that. How do they connect? Does your conclusion say more than your evidence shows?

---

## 8. Significance: "Am I focusing on what matters most?"
It's possible to think very carefully about something that doesn't matter much.

- **Weak:** A project group spending the whole meeting choosing the poster font.
- **Stronger:** Deciding the main message first, then the design.

**Ask:** Of everything here, what matters most? If you got this part perfect, how much difference would it make?

---

## 9. Fairness: "Am I being fair to the other side?"
Sometimes thinking gets bent towards the answer we already wanted.

- **Weak:** In a debate: "The other side just wants students to suffer."
- **Stronger:** "The other side thinks more homework gives us more practice. Here's why I still disagree ..."

**Ask:** Do you want this to come out a certain way? Would the other side agree you described their view fairly? Whose interests have been left out?

---
