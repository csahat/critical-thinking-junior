# The Eight Thinking Moves
Every piece of thinking has these eight parts. Each one is a place where thinking can go wrong, so each one is a question worth asking.

Introduce a move the first time it comes up with its friendly name, what it means, and one example. Prefer an example from the student's own topic. The examples below are ready to use or to adapt. The "Goes wrong like" example is useful as a hint when the student is stuck.

The order below is a reference, not a route. See `entry-points.md`.

---

## 1. What's my goal?
*Grown-up name: Purpose*

**What it means:** What you are trying to achieve. Finish the sentence: "I'm doing this so that ..."

**Example:** Choosing a CCA. "I'm choosing a CCA so that I can make new friends and get fitter." Now each CCA can be judged against those two things, instead of just picking the one that sounds cool.

**Goes wrong like:** Opening YouTube to watch a video on fractions for homework, and an hour later watching Minecraft builds. The goal got lost.

**Questions:**
- What are you trying to achieve? Say it in one sentence.
- Whose goal is this: yours, your teacher's, your parents', your group's?
- Is the goal realistic for the time you have?

---

## 2. What exactly am I trying to answer?
*Grown-up name: Question at Issue*

**What it means:** The exact question your thinking is trying to settle. A fuzzy question can't get a clear answer.

**Example:** "Is bubble tea bad?" is fuzzy. "Is drinking bubble tea every day bad for a teenager's health?" can actually be answered.

**Goes wrong like:** Two friends arguing whether Messi or Ronaldo is "the best". One means most skilful, the other means most goals scored. They are answering two different questions.

**Three kinds of question:**
- **Fact:** has one right answer you can look up or measure. *"How tall is Bukit Timah Hill?"* Go and check a reliable source.
- **Preference:** depends on taste, no wrong answer. *"Is chicken rice nicer than laksa?"*
- **Judgement:** needs reasons and evidence, and people can reasonably disagree. *"Should schools ban phones during recess?"* This kind is where thinking moves help most.

**Questions:**
- Can you write the exact question you're trying to answer?
- Would a friend read your question and know what a good answer looks like?
- Is this actually two or three questions mixed together? Which one matters most?
- Is it a fact, preference or judgement question?

---

## 3. What's my evidence?
*Grown-up name: Information*

**What it means:** The facts, data and observations your thinking is built on, and where they came from.

**Example:** In a Science practical you claim plants grow faster with music. Your evidence is the heights you measured of plants with music and without music, every day for two weeks.

**Goes wrong like:** Searching only "why video games are good for you" and finding lots of articles that agree. You only looked where agreement would be found.

**Where it came from matters:** A WhatsApp forward, a TikTok video, a textbook and a government health website are not equally reliable.

**Questions:**
- What evidence do you have? Just the facts, not what you think they mean.
- Where did it come from? How would that source know?
- What evidence would show you're wrong? Did you look for it?
- What's missing that you would want to know?

---

## 4. What did I conclude, and is that the only possibility?
*Grown-up name: Inferences*

**What it means:** The step from what you saw to what you think it means. People often mix up what they saw with what they guessed.

**Example:** You see the ground is wet in the morning and conclude "it rained last night". It could also be that the void deck was washed, or the plants were watered. The wet ground is what you saw. "It rained" is the conclusion.

**Goes wrong like:** "She's angry at me." What actually happened: she hasn't replied in the class chat since this morning. Maybe her phone was taken away, or she's at tuition.

**Questions:**
- What did you see, and what did you decide it means?
- Where does what you saw end and your guess begin?
- Can you think of a different explanation for the same evidence?
- If your conclusion is wrong, what changes?

---

## 5. What do my key words mean?
*Grown-up name: Concepts*

**What it means:** The important words or ideas in your thinking. Many arguments happen because two people use the same word to mean different things.

**Example:** A History question: "Was Raffles a good leader?" What does "good leader" mean? Good for British trade? Good for the people already living here? Good at planning a city? The answer changes depending on the meaning.

**Goes wrong like:** Your project group agrees to "keep the poster simple". One person means few words, another means plain colours, a third means less effort.

**Questions:**
- What's the most important word in your idea? What does it mean to you?
- Would your friend, teacher or parent define it the same way?
- Is there a better word that describes what you mean?

---

## 6. What am I taking for granted?
*Grown-up name: Assumptions*

**What it means:** Things you believe without noticing that you believe them. They feel so obvious that you never check them.

**Example:** "I'll finish the project the night before. It only takes two hours." This takes for granted that the printer works, your groupmates send their parts on time, and nothing else is due that night.

**Goes wrong like:** Planning to meet friends at the mall right after school, taking for granted that everyone's CCA ends at the same time.

**Questions:**
- What are you taking for granted here?
- What has to be true for your idea to work?
- If one of those things turns out to be false, does your whole idea fall apart?
- What are you assuming about how other people will behave?

---

## 7. Whose view is missing?
*Grown-up name: Point of View*

**What it means:** Everyone sees a situation from where they stand. Your view shows you some things and hides others.

**Example:** "Should the school canteen stop selling sugary drinks?" A student might see less choice. The drinks stall owner sees less income. Parents might see healthier children. Each view is real, and a good argument deals with more than one.

**Goes wrong like:** The tallest student in class deciding how high to put up the noticeboard.

**Questions:**
- Who else is affected by this?
- Who would disagree with you? What is their best reason, said in a way they'd agree is fair?
- What can you see easily because of who you are? What might you miss?

---

## 8. What happens if I'm right, or wrong?
*Grown-up name: Implications*

**What it means:** What follows from your idea, including the results you didn't plan for.

**Example:** Joining a CCA that trains four times a week. If it goes well: fitter, new friends, maybe competitions. Also: less time for homework, and being tired during test weeks.

**Goes wrong like:** Getting a hamster without working out who cleans its cage when you're away at school camp.

**Questions:**
- If you're right, what happens next? Name one good result and one not-so-good result.
- If you're wrong, what does it cost, and who pays?
- What happens in a month? By the end of the year?
- If you change your mind later, can you undo it?
